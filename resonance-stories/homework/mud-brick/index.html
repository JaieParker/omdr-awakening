Half the world lives in earth

When you dig in the garden, you are touching one of the oldest building materials on Earth. For more than 10,000 years, people everywhere have reached down, scooped up the ground beneath their feet, and turned it into a home. Even today, about one in every three people in the world lives in a house made of earth.

Why mud? Because it is everywhere, it is free, and it is clever. Thick earth walls do something magical: in the daytime they soak up the sun's heat and stay cool inside, and at night they slowly let that warmth back out. A mud house is cool in the desert afternoon and cosy in the cold night. It muffles noise, it doesn't burn, and when a mud house is no longer needed, it simply melts back into the ground it came from.

The Great Builder's secret: there is no single “best” way to build. A good home listens to four things — the people who live in it, the land around it, the weather it must stand up to, and the materials the Earth gives nearby. Every home in this book is a different answer to those same four questions.

How to use this book

First, meet the eight ways of making an earth home — the builder's toolkit. Then travel the world and visit 24 real earth homes, from the very oldest villages to a mud-brick house built in Australia. Each one tells you where it is, who lived there, what the weather is like, and how its builders were clever. At the end, there's a page to help you choose which kind of home you might build as your model.

Every fact in this book was checked against real sources (listed at the back). The photographs are all freely-shared pictures, with their photographers credited on the credits page.

Part One
The Eight Ways
of Making an Earth Home

Before you can build, you need to know the builder's tricks. Every mud home in the world is made using one (or a mix) of these eight methods.

The Builder's Toolkit — 1 to 4

1. Adobe — sun-dried bricks

stacked

Mud and straw are pressed into a wooden mould to make a brick, then tipped out and left in the sun to bake hard — no oven needed. The dry bricks are stacked into walls and glued with more mud. This is the most common earth method on Earth. Adobe is an old Arabic-Spanish word for mud brick.

2. Cob — mud loaves, no bricks

shaped

No bricks at all. Sticky mud, straw and water are trodden together (often by bare feet!) into big lumps called cobs. The builder stacks the lumps by hand and squishes them into one thick, curvy wall, then trims it smooth with a spade. Slow, but wonderfully strong.

3. Rammed earth (pisé)

packed

Slightly damp earth is poured into a strong wooden box (a form) and then pounded down hard with a heavy tamper, layer by layer, until it is as solid as soft rock. Lift the box away and the wall stands on its own. You can often see the stripey layers. Some rammed-earth walls have lasted over a thousand years.

4. Wattle and daub

woven & layered

First a lattice of thin sticks or bamboo is woven between wooden posts (the wattle). Then sticky mud mixed with straw and even animal dung is smeared all over it and left to dry (the daub). The sticks hold the mud; the mud fills the gaps. One of the oldest tricks of all.

The Builder's Toolkit — 5 to 8

5. Compressed earth block

pressed

A newer, modern cousin of adobe. Instead of drying in the sun, damp earth is squeezed very hard in a metal press (worked by a lever) to make a neat, dense block straightaway. The blocks are so even they stack like toy bricks. Strong, fast, and still just earth.

6. Mud plaster & render

layered

Almost every earth home wears a coat. A smooth layer of fine mud (sometimes mixed with dung or lime) is spread over the walls like icing on a cake. It fills cracks, keeps rain from washing the wall away, and can be re-smeared every year. In many places re-plastering the house is a job for the whole village.

7. The Nubian vault

fitted

A brilliant desert trick from Sudan, where there is almost no wood. The builder leans sun-dried bricks against a back wall at a slight angle, so each brick holds up the next, and builds a curved roof that supports itself — with no wooden frame at all. The result is a cool, cave-like vaulted home.

8. Puddled mud & sod (turf)

packed & piled

The simplest of all. Puddled mud means piling wet earth up by hand in courses, letting each layer dry before adding the next — a wall with no bricks and no mould. Sod (or turf) goes further: the builder slices the grassy ground itself into heavy blocks, roots and all, and stacks them like bricks. Used where the earth is held together by grass roots.

The very first mud-brick home? The oldest ones we know of were the round houses of Jericho, built by the world's first farmers about 11,000 years ago (around 9,000 BC) — older than the pyramids, older than writing, older than the wheel. Their bricks were shaped by hand like little loaves of clay, long before anyone invented the brick mould. Two other very early villages, Çayönü in Turkey and Tell Aswad in Syria, are almost as old.
1about 10,500 years ago

Jericho Round House

📍 Jordan Valley, Palestine — the world's oldest town
Left: the real dried-mud and stone ruins at Tell es-Sultan, Jericho.   Right: what one round home looked like.

🔍 Spot it!

  • A Walls of hand-shaped mud bricks, dried in the sun
  • B Roof of brushwood smeared with mud
  • C One small, low doorway
  • D Round shape — strong and easy to build

📖 The story

Jericho is one of the oldest towns on Earth. Its people were some of the first farmers ever — they had just learned to grow wheat, so instead of following the animals they hunted, they stayed in one place and needed homes that lasted.

They had no brick moulds yet, so they squeezed clay and straw into bricks by hand and baked them in the hot sun. Round walls were quick to build and didn't need strong corners. The thick mud kept the house cool in the blazing valley heat.

Who: the world's first farmers
Climate: hot, dry desert oasis
Built with: hand-shaped mud bricks
Land: a green spring in dry hills
Roof: brushwood + mud
Method: adobe (sun-dried)
Wow! Jericho's people built a huge stone tower over 8 metres tall more than 10,000 years ago — long before metal tools or writing existed!
2about 9,000 years ago

Çatalhöyük Roof-Door House

📍 Konya Plain, Turkey — a town with no streets
Left: the excavated mud-brick walls, packed together with no gaps.   Right: how you entered — through the roof!

🔍 Spot it!

  • A Flat roofs were the streets — people walked on top
  • B A hole + ladder to climb down inside
  • C No doors on the ground at all
  • D Houses share walls, packed like a honeycomb

📖 The story

Thousands of people lived here squeezed together with no streets between the houses. To get home you climbed onto the rooftops, walked across town along the roofs, and dropped down a ladder through a hole into your house.

Why build so strangely? Packing the mud houses tightly made them strong and warm, and having no low doors kept out the cold wind, floods and wild animals. The roof hole also let the cooking smoke escape.

Who: early farming families
Climate: hot summers, cold winters
Built with: mud bricks + plaster
Land: a flat grassy plain
Door: a hole in the roof!
Method: adobe (sun-dried)
Wow! Families painted pictures on their walls and even buried loved ones under the floors of their own homes.
3about 5,000 years ago

Sumerian Mud & Reed House

📍 Southern Iraq — the land between two rivers
Left: a real reed guesthouse in the Iraqi marshes, built the same way for 5,000 years.   Right: the reed-arch shape.

🔍 Spot it!

  • A Giant reed bundles bent into arches
  • B Walls of woven reed mats
  • C Thick mud-brick homes nearby for winter
  • D A curved reed roof — the first archways!

📖 The story

The Sumerians built some of the world's first cities, like Ur and Uruk. There were few trees and no stone, but two things were everywhere: river clay and tall marsh reeds. So they used both.

Richer families pressed clay into mud bricks and built rooms around a shady courtyard. Marsh families tied bundles of reeds and bent them into arches. People think bending those reeds into curves may have given humans the very first idea for the arch — a shape we still build with today.

Who: the Sumerians
Climate: very hot, dry + marshes
Built with: mud brick & reeds
Land: flat river plains
Shape: courtyard + reed arch
Method: adobe + reed
Wow! The Marsh Arabs of Iraq still build these tall reed houses today — almost exactly as the Sumerians did 5,000 years ago.

Egyptian Tomb-Builder's House

📍 Deir el-Medina, Egypt — by the Valley of the Kings
Left: the real mud-brick village walls still standing in the desert near Luxor.   Right: one whitewashed home.

🔍 Spot it!

  • A Mud-brick walls painted white to bounce off the sun
  • B Small windows set up high for shade
  • C A stone base to keep out damp

📖 The story

This tidy walled village was home to the craftsmen who carved and painted the pharaohs' secret tombs, and their families. The government even built the houses for them in neat rows.

In the fierce Egyptian sun, they were clever: white paint reflected the heat, and windows up high let in light without the harsh glare. On hot nights the whole family climbed the inside stairs to sleep up on the cool flat roof under the stars.

Who: royal tomb craftsmen
Climate: hot desert, cool nights
Built with: sun-dried mud bricks
Land: a bowl in bare hills
Walls: plastered + painted white
Method: adobe (sun-dried)
Wow! These villagers left so many notes on stone that we know their names and arguments — and they went on one of history's first-ever workers' strikes!
5a 3,000-year-old idea

Nubian Vault House

📍 Sudan & southern Egypt — along the River Nile
Left: a real curved Nubian vault at the Nubia Museum, Aswan.   Right: the leaning-brick roof shape.

🔍 Spot it!

  • A Bricks leaned at a slant, each holding the next
  • B A special curve (like a hanging chain) — super strong
  • C A thick earth roof that stays cool inside
  • D Built with no wooden frame at all

📖 The story

In the Nubian desert there is almost no wood, so builders couldn't make a wooden frame to hold up a roof while it dried. They invented a genius trick instead: lean the mud bricks against a back wall at a slight angle, so each new brick rests on the one before, curving up and over until the roof closes itself.

The curve they use is called a catenary — the exact shape a chain makes when it hangs. It carries its own weight perfectly, which is why some of these vaults have stood for over 3,000 years.

Who: Nubian master masons
Climate: hot, dry desert
Built with: sun-dried mud bricks
Land: sandy Nile riverbanks
Roof: self-supporting vault
Method: Nubian vault
Wow! The oldest Nubian vault still standing in Egypt is about 3,300 years old — older than most castles in the world!

Djenné Earth Buildings

📍 Djenné, Mali — West Africa, by the Bani River
Left: the Great Mosque of Djenné — the largest mud-brick building in the world.   Right: the wall shape.

🔍 Spot it!

  • A Wooden toron sticks poking out of the walls
  • B Smooth mud plaster (called banco)
  • C Thick walls that taper thinner near the top
  • D Tall towers topped with little pinnacles

📖 The story

Djenné's homes and its famous mosque are built from mud bricks called ferey, coated in smooth mud plaster. The town has a short but very heavy rainy season, and each year the rain washes a little of the mud away.

So the builders had a brilliant idea: leave wooden sticks sticking out all over the walls. They aren't just decoration — they are a permanent ladder. Once a year the whole town holds a festival and climbs the sticks to re-plaster the building together, keeping it strong for another year.

Who: the whole community
Climate: hot, dry + heavy rains
Built with: ferey mud bricks
Land: a flat river floodplain
Trick: toron sticks = a ladder
Method: adobe + mud plaster
Wow! The Great Mosque of Djenné is the biggest mud-brick building on the whole planet, and became a World Heritage treasure in 1988.
7a very old tradition

Musgum "Shell" Mud Hut

📍 Northern Cameroon — near Lake Maga
Left: a real Musgum tolek in Cameroon with its ridged mud walls.   Right: the tall shell shape.

🔍 Spot it!

  • A Herringbone ridges — footholds and rain-drains
  • B A hole at the top for smoke & cool air
  • C Extra-thick walls at the bottom, thinner up high
  • D A tall dome shape up to 9 metres high

📖 The story

The Musgum people build these tall, pointed homes from just mud, water and thatch — no bricks and almost no wood. They pile the mud up in rings, thick at the bottom for strength and thinner toward the top, curving into a shape like a giant shell or artillery shell (its nickname is case obus).

Those criss-cross ridges running up the outside are the clever part: they help rain run off quickly so it doesn't soak the wall, and they work as footholds so people can climb up to re-smooth the mud each year.

Who: the Musgum people
Climate: hot, with flooding rains
Built with: mud, water & thatch
Land: flat grassy plains
Shape: tall pointed shell
Method: puddled mud coils
Wow! The hole at the very top isn't just for smoke — it can be an emergency escape hatch if floodwater rises inside!

Kassena Painted House

📍 Tiébélé, Burkina Faso — West African savanna
Left: real painted patterns on a Kassena house in Tiébélé.   Right: the low, decorated wall.

🔍 Spot it!

  • A Bold black, white & red painted patterns
  • B Tiny windows to keep out heat and light
  • C A very low, small doorway
  • D Walls polished smooth and varnished

📖 The story

In this village the men build the thick mud walls in the dry season, and the women do all the painting — passing the patterns down from mother to daughter for hundreds of years. The colours come from the earth itself: red from laterite soil, white from chalk, black from charcoal.

After painting, they polish the walls with smooth stones and brush on a natural varnish made from néré-bean pods, which helps the rain slide off. The tiny windows and low doors keep the fierce savanna heat out and the inside cool and dark.

Who: the Kassena people
Climate: hot savanna + rains
Built with: earth, straw & dung
Land: flat, dry grassland
Colours: from soil, chalk & charcoal
Method: painted mud plaster
Wow! The patterns aren't just pretty — each shape tells a story, and the colours have meanings: red for courage, white for honesty, black for the night.
9towers mostly after 1533

Shibam Mud Towers

📍 Wadi Hadhramaut, Yemen — the "Manhattan of the Desert"
Left: the real mud-brick skyscrapers of Shibam.   Right: tall towers packed side by side.

🔍 Spot it!

  • A Mud towers 5 to 11 storeys tall — up to 30 m!
  • B Small windows to keep out the desert sun
  • C White lime tops that protect the mud from rain
  • D Built up, not out, to save space and stay safe

📖 The story

Shibam sits on a trade route in a flat desert valley. Long ago, families needed to be safe from raiders and from the floods that sometimes rushed down the valley — so instead of spreading out, they built straight up.

Every tower is made entirely of mud bricks, some as tall as an 11-storey building. The thick earth walls keep the rooms cool in the baking heat, and the lower floors (with no windows) were used for storing grain and keeping animals, while families lived up high where it was cooler and safer.

Who: Hadhrami trading families
Climate: very hot, almost no rain
Built with: mud bricks + plaster
Land: a flat valley by cliffs
Height: up to 11 storeys
Method: adobe (sun-dried)
Wow! Shibam has some of the oldest skyscrapers in the world — and every single one is made of mud! It became a World Heritage Site in 1982.

Yazd Desert House

📍 Yazd, Iran — between two great deserts
Left: real wind-catcher towers rising above Yazd's rooftops.   Right: how a badgir cools the house.

🔍 Spot it!

  • A A badgir wind-catcher tower — natural cooling
  • B Thick mud walls that block the heat
  • C Domed roofs that shed the sun's rays

📖 The story

Yazd is one of the biggest cities on Earth built almost entirely of mud brick (adobe). Summers here roar past 40°C with almost no rain, so staying cool is everything.

Long before electric fans, the Yazdi people invented air-conditioning made of mud. Tall towers called badgirs stand above the roofs and catch even the smallest breeze, funnelling it down into the rooms below to cool the family. The thick earth walls do the rest, keeping the fierce daytime heat outside.

Who: the Yazdi people
Climate: scorching desert, no rain
Built with: adobe mud bricks
Land: flat desert plains
Cooling: wind-catcher towers
Method: adobe (sun-dried)
Wow! A wind-catcher is like a chimney working in reverse — it grabs the breeze from the sky and pours cool air down onto the family below.

Arg-e Bam Citadel

📍 Bam, Iran — a whole city made of mud
Left: the great mud citadel of Bam, photographed before the 2003 earthquake.   Right: its walls and towers.

Arg-e Bam isn't one house — it's an entire walled city built of mud, standing on the old Silk Road where traders passed through. Inside its giant walls were homes, stables, army barracks and even a ruler's palace, all made of earth.

Before 2003 it was probably the largest mud-brick building in the whole world. Sadly a big earthquake shook much of it down in 2003 — and people have been carefully rebuilding this earthen city ever since, using the very same mud methods as the ancient builders.

Who: traders, soldiers & rulers

Aït Benhaddou Kasbahs

📍 Southern Morocco — on the edge of the Sahara
Left: the real earthen kasbahs of Aït Benhaddou climbing the hill.   Right: rammed-earth towers.

🔍 Spot it!

  • A Walls of pisé — earth pounded hard in moulds
  • B Tall corner towers with notched tops
  • C Patterns pressed into the upper walls

📖 The story

Aït Benhaddou is a ksar — a fortified village of tall earthen houses called kasbahs, built where a caravan route crossed the river below the Atlas Mountains. Merchants needed their homes to be safe, so they built thick, tower-topped walls.

The walls are rammed earth: damp earth and straw pounded hard inside wooden moulds until solid. It's a hot, dry place, but when the rare rain does come it slowly wears the earth away — so families must repair the walls again and again to keep the village standing.

Who: merchants & traders
Climate: hot & dry, edge of Sahara
Built with: rammed earth + adobe
Land: a dry mountain river valley
Shape: fortified hillside village

Wow! This earthen village is a real movie star — it's been the backdrop for Gladiator, Lawrence of Arabia and Game of Thrones!

Mohenjo-daro City House

📍 Sindh, Pakistan — beside the Indus River
Left: the real brick ruins of Mohenjo-daro, over 4,000 years old.   Right: a courtyard house.

🔍 Spot it!

  • A Every brick made the same size across the city
  • B An open courtyard (yard) in the middle for shade
  • C Homes up to two storeys tall
  • D Covered drains — an early sewer system!

📖 The story

Mohenjo-daro was one of the world's first carefully planned cities, built by the Indus Valley people. Their streets were laid out in a neat grid, and their houses turned inward around a private courtyard to stay cool and quiet.

They were amazing brick-makers. They used sun-dried mud bricks and tough fired bricks (baked hard in a fire so water can't wash them away), and made every brick almost the same size — like a giant ancient LEGO set. The city even had over 700 wells and covered drains along the streets.

Who: the Indus Valley people
Climate: hot, dry river plain
Built with: mud + fired bricks
Land: a flat ridge by the river
Clever bit: drains & 700 wells
Method: adobe + fired brick
Wow! Every brick was made almost exactly the same size across the whole city — like a giant ancient LEGO set with matching pieces!
14oldest from 1371

Fujian Tulou Roundhouse

📍 Fujian, China — green mountain country
Left: real round tulou earth buildings in Fujian.   Right: one giant roundhouse for a whole clan.

🔍 Spot it!

  • A Rammed-earth walls up to 1.8 m thick
  • B One big strong gate — the only way in
  • C Windows only high up (to keep out bandits)
  • D A giant ring around a shared courtyard

📖 The story

A tulou is not one home — it's a whole village in a single round building! The Hakka people built these huge earthen rings so that an entire family clan — sometimes up to 800 people — could all live together and stay safe.

The walls are rammed earth (damp earth pounded hard between wooden boards), so thick and strong that bandits couldn't break in. Everyone's rooms face a friendly shared courtyard in the middle, and every family's rooms are the same size — so nobody is above anyone else.

Who: the Hakka people
Climate: warm, wet, subtropical
Built with: rammed earth + wood
Land: green hills & rice terraces
Holds: up to 800 people!
Method: rammed earth
Wow! Builders sometimes mixed sticky rice into the earth to help the walls set as hard as rock — a cake recipe that makes a fortress!
153,000+ years old

Yaodong Cave Home

📍 Loess Plateau, Northern China
Left: real yaodong cave homes cut into a Chinese hillside.   Right: arched rooms dug into the cliff.

🔍 Spot it!

  • A Arched rooms dug into the hillside — the home is the hill
  • B Thick earth all around keeps it warm & cool
  • C Soft yellow loess soil (easy to dig, holds its shape)
  • D A pretty lattice window & door at the front

📖 The story

Here the builders didn't stack anything up at all — they dug their home into the ground! Northern China's Loess Plateau is made of deep, soft yellow soil called loess (say "luss") — a big word meaning dusty earth blown into place by the wind long ago. It's soft enough to dig, but firm enough to hold its shape.

Diggers carve an arched room straight into a cliff, or dig a square sunken yard and cut rooms into its sides. The thick earth all around works like a cosy blanket (called insulation) — warm in freezing winters, cool in hot summers, with almost no heating needed.

Who: country families
Climate: cold winters, hot summers
Built with: the earth itself — no bricks
Land: soft yellow loess cliffs
Clever bit: earth = free heating
Method: dug-out cave (loess)
Wow! Tens of millions of people still live in cosy cave homes like these — snug as a hobbit house, and cool as air-conditioning in summer!
16a living Himalayan tradition

Ladakhi Mountain House

📍 Ladakh, India — high in the Himalaya
Left: real flat-roofed mountain houses at Lamayuru, Ladakh.   Right: a whitewashed home.

🔍 Spot it!

  • A Thick mud walls that lean in as they rise (a "batter")
  • B A flat earth roof for drying hay & sleeping
  • C Walls whitewashed bright white
  • D A strong stone base under the mud

📖 The story

Ladakh is a cold desert high in the mountains — above 3,000 metres, freezing in winter, with hardly any rain and very few trees. So people build with what there is: sun-dried mud bricks and rammed earth, on a base of stone.

The thick earth walls insulate so well that a room can stay warm and cosy (near 20°C) even when it's −20°C outside! The walls slope gently inward — a "batter" — to make them stronger, and the flat roof becomes an extra outdoor room for stacking winter hay and sleeping under the stars.

Who: Ladakhi mountain people
Climate: freezing cold desert
Built with: adobe + rammed earth
Land: dry, treeless high valleys
Roof: flat, for hay & sleeping
Method: adobe + rammed earth
Wow! The mud walls are such good insulators that it can be cosy inside at 20°C while it's a freezing −20°C outside — a 40-degree difference!
17about 1,000 years old

Taos Pueblo

📍 New Mexico, USA — below the Rocky Mountains
Left: the real many-storeyed adobe homes of Taos Pueblo, lived in for 1,000 years.   Right: the terraced shape.

🔍 Spot it!

  • A Adobe rooms stacked up to five storeys high
  • B Wooden ladders to climb between levels
  • C Roof beams (vigas) poking out of the walls
  • D Thick mud walls, re-plastered by hand each year

📖 The story

The Taos people have lived in this adobe town for about a thousand years — right up to today, making it one of the oldest lived-in homes in all of America. It's built of adobe: earth and straw mixed with water and dried hard in the sun.

The rooms are piled side by side and on top of each other like giant earthy building blocks. Long ago the bottom rooms had no doors at all — families climbed ladders and went in through the roof, pulling the ladders up to stay safe. The roof beams, called vigas (a big word for the wooden poles that hold up the roof), still poke out of the walls.

Who: the Taos (Puebloan) people
Climate: hot summers, snowy winters
Built with: adobe + wooden vigas
Land: a dry valley by mountains
Height: up to 5 storeys
Method: adobe (sun-dried)
Wow! To keep the old village just as it was, the Taos people still don't allow electricity or running water in the ancient houses — it's a World Heritage Site.

Pueblo Bonito Great House

📍 Chaco Canyon, New Mexico, USA
Left: a real bird's-eye view of the huge D-shaped Pueblo Bonito.   Right: its plan, seen from above.

🔍 Spot it!

  • A About 800 rooms in one giant building
  • B Round underground rooms called kivas
  • C Stone blocks glued with adobe (mud) mortar
  • D A huge "D" shape with a long straight front

📖 The story

Pueblo Bonito was a "great house" — an enormous building with around 800 rooms, rising four or five storeys, made by the Ancestral Puebloan people about a thousand years ago in a dry desert canyon.

They built it with a clever "core-and-veneer" method: a rubble core faced with neat sandstone, all held together with adobe (mud) mortar — the earthy "glue" between the stones. (Mortar is a big word for the soft paste that fills the gaps and sticks the blocks together.) It grew into a giant "D" shape over hundreds of years.

Who: the Ancestral Puebloans
Climate: very dry high desert
Built with: sandstone + adobe mortar
Land: a rocky sandstone canyon
Size: around 800 rooms
Method: stone + adobe mortar
Wow! Building Pueblo Bonito took an estimated 805,000 hours of work — and in one room, diggers found eight ancient wooden flutes!

Spanish-Mexican Adobe Casa

📍 Santa Fe, New Mexico & Mexico
Left: the real adobe front of Santa Fe's Palace of the Governors (built 1610).   Right: a flat-roofed casa.

🔍 Spot it!

  • A Thick adobe walls, coated in mud plaster or stucco
  • B A flat roof with little water-spouts (canales)
  • C Wooden roof beams (vigas) across the top
  • D A shady covered porch along the front

📖 The story

When Spanish settlers came to the dry American Southwest, they learned adobe building from the Pueblo peoples already living there, and mixed it with their own ideas from Spain. The result is the cosy flat-roofed casa (Spanish for "house").

Sun-dried adobe bricks make thick walls that stay cool in the day and warm at night. A coat of plaster or stucco (a hard outer coating) protects the mud from rain, and little spouts called canales let rainwater shoot off the flat roof so it doesn't soak in. Santa Fe's Palace of the Governors has been used since 1610!

Who: Spanish & Mexican settlers
Climate: dry — hot days, cold nights
Built with: adobe + wooden vigas
Land: dusty desert river valleys
Roof: flat, with water spouts
Method: adobe (sun-dried)
Wow! The Palace of the Governors has been in use since 1610 — that makes it older than almost every other public building in the whole USA!

Prairie Sod House ("Soddy")

📍 The Great Plains, USA — Nebraska & Kansas
Left: a real Nebraska family in front of their sod house in 1886.   Right: stacked turf blocks.

🔍 Spot it!

  • A Walls of sod — blocks of grass with its roots & soil
  • B Blocks stacked grass-side down, like heavy bricks
  • C A roof of branches, brush and more sod on top
  • D Very thick walls — warm, cool, and won't burn

📖 The story

When pioneer families moved onto the wide American prairie, there were almost no trees and no stone to build with — just endless grass. So they built with the grass itself! Sod (also called turf) is a big word for a slab of earth held together by thick, tangled grass roots.

Settlers cut the sod into heavy blocks and stacked them grass-side down, like bricks, to make walls. The thick earthy walls kept the little house cosy in blizzards and cool in summer, and sod doesn't catch fire. The trouble was rain — a sod roof could leak muddy water, so it needed constant repair.

Who: pioneer homesteaders
Climate: hot summers, icy winters
Built with: blocks of grassy turf
Land: flat, treeless prairie
Good bits: warm & fireproof
Method: sod / turf blocks
Wow! Sod walls were so thick and cosy that families stayed warm in blizzards — but sometimes bugs, mice, and even snakes lived right inside the earthy walls!

Devon Cob Cottage

📍 Devon, England — the green West Country
Left: a real thatched cob cottage in Dawlish, Devon.   Right: its thick walls and straw roof.

🔍 Spot it!

  • A Very thick cob walls (mud + straw), often 60 cm!
  • B A thatched roof — bundled straw or reed — the "good hat"
  • C A stone base — the "good pair of shoes"
  • D Soft, curvy walls shaped by hand

📖 The story

In rainy Devon, good stone and timber were hard to get, so country families built with the earth under their feet. Cob is a mix of clay, straw and water, piled up in layers by hand and trodden firm — no bricks, no moulds. The walls come out lovely and curvy.

There's an old Devon saying: "All cob wants is a good hat and a good pair of shoes." The "hat" is a wide thatched roof (a thick roof made of bundled straw) to keep rain off the top, and the "shoes" are a stone base to keep the wall off the wet ground. Kept dry like that, cob lasts for centuries.

Who: English country families
Climate: cool, wet and rainy
Built with: cob (clay + straw)
Land: green rolling farmland
Roof: thatched straw/reed
Method: cob
Wow! There are still around 20,000 cob cottages standing in Devon today — and some are over 500 years old!

French Pisé Farmhouse

📍 Near Lyon, France — the Rhône valley
Left: a real rammed-earth (pisé) farmhouse near Lyon.   Right: notice the stripey earth layers.

🔍 Spot it!

  • A Rammed-earth walls with faint stripey layers
  • B A wide roof overhang to keep rain off the walls
  • C A stone base to stop damp rising up
  • D Wooden shutters over the windows

📖 The story

Around the French city of Lyon, farmers found their soil was a perfect mix of gravel, sand and clay — just right for building. So they made pisé (rammed earth): they poured damp earth into a wooden mould and pounded it hard, layer by layer, until it set as solid as soft rock.

You can often still see the faint stripes where each pounded layer sits on the one below. Like cob, pisé needs a stone base and a good roof overhang to stay dry. Earth was dug from right beside the house — and the holes left behind sometimes filled with water to become farm ponds!

Who: French farming families
Climate: warm summers, cool winters
Built with: gravelly rammed earth
Land: farmland & river valleys
Look: stripey pounded layers
Method: rammed earth (pisé)
Wow! Lyon is one of the only big cities in Europe with rammed-earth buildings dotted all over town — even in the suburbs!

Alhambra Rammed-Earth Walls

📍 Granada, Spain — "the red one"
Left: the real rammed-earth walls of the Alhambra's fortress in Granada.   Right: its red earth walls & tower.

🔍 Spot it!

  • A Tapia walls — earth pounded hard in moulds
  • B Lime mixed in to make them super strong
  • C Tall, thick fortress towers
  • D Reddish earth — it gave the palace its name

📖 The story

High on a rocky hill above Granada stands the famous Alhambra palace-fortress, built by Spain's Moorish rulers about 800 years ago. Its mighty walls are rammed earth, called tapia — but with a secret ingredient.

The builders mixed in lime (a white powder made from heated limestone), which made this "royal" earth so tough it didn't even need a plaster coat. The reddish colour of the local earth is why the whole place is called Alhambra — Arabic for "the red one." Those earthen walls have survived for nearly 800 years.

Who: Spain's Moorish rulers
Climate: warm, dry Mediterranean
Built with: earth + lime (tapia)
Land: a rocky hilltop
Secret: lime makes it rock-hard
Method: rammed earth (tapia)
Wow! The famous red walls of the Alhambra are made of rammed earth — and millions of people visit this World Heritage wonder every year.

Eltham Mud-Brick Home

📍 Eltham, Victoria, Australia — our local one!
Left: real mud-brick artist houses at Montsalvat in Eltham, the colony that started it all.   Right: a bush home.

🔍 Spot it!

  • A Walls of mud bricks (adobe) from local soil
  • B Wide roof eaves (overhangs) to shelter the walls
  • C Big recycled timber beams
  • D Nestled among Australian gum trees & bush

📖 The story

Here's one from right here in Australia! After World War II, a self-taught builder named Alistair Knox started making mud-brick homes around Eltham, near Melbourne — inspired by a nearby artists' colony called Montsalvat. It began a whole Australian mud-brick movement.

Knox made his bricks from local soil and used lots of recycled timber, brick and slate. He believed a house should sit gently and naturally in the bush around it, with wide eaves to shade the earth walls from sun and rain. He designed over 1,000 buildings — and mud-brick homes are still built in Eltham today.

Who: Alistair Knox & friends
Climate: warm-dry summers, cool winters
Built with: adobe + recycled timber
Land: the Australian bush
Idea: live gently with nature
Method: adobe (sun-dried)
Wow! Alistair Knox designed more than 1,000 buildings, and there's even a park named after him in Eltham — earth building is alive right near us!

📚 The Builder's Word List

Some grown-up building words appear in this book. Here's what they all mean, in plain English. Point to any word you meet and check it here!

Adobe
A brick made of mud and straw, dried hard in the sun (not in an oven).
Badgir
A "wind-catcher" tower that catches the breeze and sends cool air down into a house.
Banco
The smooth mud plaster used to coat buildings in Mali.
Batter
When a wall leans gently inward as it goes up, to make it stronger.
Canales
Little spouts that let rainwater shoot off a flat roof.
Catenary
The special curve a chain makes when it hangs — a very strong shape for arches and roofs.
Cob
A mix of clay, straw and water, stacked by hand into thick walls — no bricks.
Compressed earth block
A modern earth brick squeezed hard in a press instead of dried in the sun.
Courtyard
An open, roofless space in the middle of a house, for shade and fresh air.
Daub
Sticky mud smeared over woven sticks to make a wall (see wattle).
Eaves
The edge of a roof that sticks out past the walls, to keep rain off them.
Ferey
The name for the sun-dried mud bricks used in Djenné, Mali.
Fired brick
A brick baked hard in a very hot fire, so water can't wash it away.
Foundation
The strong base a building stands on, often made of stone.
Insulation
Anything that keeps heat in or out — thick mud walls are great insulation.
Kasbah
A tall earthen house or small fort in North Africa.
Kiva
A round room, often underground, used by Puebloan people for gatherings.
Ksar
A walled village of earthen houses in North Africa.
Laterite
A reddish soil used to make red paint.
Lime
A white powder made from heated limestone; mixed into earth or plaster to make it tougher.
Loess
Soft yellow soil blown into place by wind long ago — easy to dig, holds its shape.
Mortar
The soft paste (often mud) that fills the gaps and glues bricks or stones together.
Nubian vault
A curved mud-brick roof built with no wooden frame, by leaning the bricks.
Pisé
The French word for rammed earth.
Plaster / render
A smooth coat spread over a wall to protect it and make it neat.
Puddled mud
Wet earth piled up by hand in layers, with no bricks and no mould.
Rammed earth
Damp earth poured into a mould and pounded hard until it sets solid.
Sod / turf
A slab of earth held together by thick grass roots, cut and stacked like a brick.
Stucco
A hard outer coating spread over walls to guard them from rain.
Thatch
A thick roof made of bundled straw or reed.
Toron
The wooden sticks that poke out of Djenné's walls, used as a permanent ladder.
Viga
A wooden beam that holds up a flat earth roof (used in the American Southwest).
Wattle
A lattice of woven sticks that gets covered in mud (see daub).

🔨 Which could YOU build?

Every home in this book could become a wonderful model on your 50 cm × 50 cm base. Here are four that work especially well with real earth and natural materials — but the choice is all yours, Great Builder. Remember Anik's words: Look. Listen. Imagine. Plan. Then build.

🏠 Egyptian or Pueblo adobe house ★★★★★ easiest

Make lots of tiny mud bricks in an ice-cube tray or by hand (mud + a little straw), dry them in the sun, then stack them into walls with mud as glue. You learn exactly what real adobe builders do. Add a flat cardboard-and-mud roof. Best for: learning brick-making; a neat, strong result.

🏚 Musgum shell hut or a round house ★★★★

No bricks needed — build up rings of stiff mud (like cob) into a tall dome, thick at the bottom. Press in herringbone ridges with a stick while it's wet. Best for: sculpting with your hands; a real "wow" shape.

🌲 Devon cob cottage ★★★★

Build thick, curvy walls from mud mixed with straw on a base of little stones (its "shoes"), then add a roof of dried grass or straw thatch (its "hat"). Best for: a cosy, forgiving build — cob doesn't mind being bumpy.

🏛 Nubian vault house ★★★ a challenge!

Make mud bricks and lean them to build a curved roof with no frame. Trickier — but if it stands, it's amazing, and you'll have learned the secret of the arch. Best for: a builder who loves a puzzle. (Tip: build against a firm back wall.)

A Great Builder's promise: your first idea might not work — a wall may fall, a roof may sag. That's not failing, that's building! Just like Anik: Try. Watch what happens. Change something. Try again.
