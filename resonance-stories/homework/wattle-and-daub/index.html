Great Builders of Earth

Wattle & Daub
Houses on Stilts

How to weave a house from sticks and mud —
and lift it up on wooden legs, the way builders have for 6,000 years
“Weave the sticks. Squish the mud. Lift it off the ground.”
Researched for Arthur Parker, age 9 — Great Builder
A research companion — the choosing, planning and building of the model stay Arthur's own work.

A house you can weave

Imagine building a wall the way you'd weave a basket. You push some sticks upright into the ground, then bend long thin branches in and out between them — over, under, over, under — until you have a woven fence. Then you grab handfuls of sticky mud and squish it right into the weave, from both sides, until it's one solid wall. When it dries, it's hard, warm and strong. That is wattle and daub, and people have been building homes this way for at least 6,000 years.

Two little words: the wattle is the woven basket of sticks (the wall's skeleton). The daub is the sticky mud plaster you press into it (the wall's skin). Weave the wattle, add the daub — that's the whole idea.

Arthur's house has one more clever trick: it stands on wooden stilts. Long posts lift the whole house up off the ground. Why would you do that? To stay above floods, to keep the floor dry away from damp ground, to keep out mice and creepy-crawlies, to let cool air flow underneath, and to make a handy shady space for storing things down below. The very oldest wattle-and-daub houses we know of — over 5,000 years old, beside the lakes of the Alps — were built exactly like this: on stilts.

The Great Builder's secret: a good home listens to four things — the people who live in it, the land around it, the weather it must stand up to, and the materials nearby. Sticks, mud, straw and wood are all things you can find close to home. That is why this way of building grew up all over the world.

How to use this book

First, meet the three big ideas — wattle, daub and stilts (plus how houses are finished and roofed). Then travel the world and visit five famous styles, from a Stone-Age lake village to a Japanese farmhouse and a Colombian coffee town. After that comes the best part: a step-by-step guide to building a real wattle-and-daub house on stilts. And right at the end, a special plan for making your own 50 cm model.

Part One
The Three Big Ideas

Before you build, learn the builder's tricks. Every wattle-and-daub house on stilts is made from these ideas: weave, daub, lift — then finish and roof.

The Builder's Toolkit

1. Wattle — the woven sticks

the skeleton

Push strong upright sticks (called staves) into the frame, then weave long bendy branches (called withies — usually hazel or willow) in and out between them, just like weaving a basket. Pack them tight. Now you have a strong, holey wall — ready for mud.

2. Daub — the sticky mud

the skin

Mix clay soil, a little sand, and lots of chopped straw with water into a thick, sticky paste. Builders often trod it with their bare feet to squish it together! The straw is the secret — it holds the mud together so it doesn't crack and crumble.

3. Stilts — the wooden legs

lift it up

Strong posts (often oak) are driven deep into the ground to hold the house up high. A clever old trick: char the buried ends in a fire first — the black, burnt layer stops them rotting for many extra years. Up on the stilts, the house stays above floods, dry, and free of pests.

4. Lime wash — the raincoat

the finish

When the daub is dry and hard, builders paint it with lime wash — a thin white coat that works like a raincoat. It keeps rain off, stops mould, and — cleverly — still lets the wall breathe, so damp can escape instead of rotting the wood. This is why old wattle-and-daub houses are often white.

5. Thatch — the straw hat

the roof

The roof is usually thatch — bundles of dry straw or water reed laid in overlapping rows, thick and steep so rain runs straight off. A good reed roof can last up to 60 years! The pointed top (the ridge) wears out first and gets replaced.

Alpine Lake Village

📍 Lakes around the Alps — Switzerland, Germany, Italy, France, Austria, Slovenia
A row of huts on wooden piles beside a lake — the oldest wattle-and-daub houses we know of.

🔍 Spot it!

  • A Walls of woven sticks packed with clay — real wattle and daub
  • B Roof of poles covered with reeds or bark
  • C Tall wooden piles holding everything up
  • D Soft, marshy ground beside the lake

📖 The story

More than 5,000 years ago, farming families around the Alps built villages of little huts up on wooden stilts. They wove walls from sticks and packed them with clay — exactly what Arthur is making.

They built on stilts to stay above the floods from the lakes and rivers. Amazingly, many of the wooden posts fell into the water and never rotted — so we can still study them today, and even count the tree-rings to find out exactly which year they were cut.

Who: Stone-Age & Bronze-Age farmers
Land: marshy shores beside lakes
Walls: wattle woven + packed with clay
On stilts? Yes — against floods
Roof: poles + reeds / bark
Method: wattle and daub
Wow! There are 111 of these protected sites, and they are so important they became a UNESCO World Heritage treasure in 2011 — the same list as the Pyramids!
2about 1450–1650

English Tudor House

📍 England — the famous “black-and-white” houses
The dark stripes are the oak frame; the white panels between them are wattle and daub, painted with lime wash.

🔍 Spot it!

  • A White wattle-and-daub panels between the timbers
  • B Big oak frame doing all the holding-up
  • C Upper floor sticking out over the lower one (a “jetty”)
  • D Strong wooden pegs instead of nails

📖 The story

In Tudor England, builders made a strong oak frame first — joined with wooden pegs, no nails — then filled every gap with wattle and daub and painted it white with lime wash.

Here's a surprise: the famous black-and-white look is mostly a much later idea! In Tudor times the panels were often painted cream, pink or even red. People painted the timbers black hundreds of years afterwards, and the old oak also darkens all by itself.

Who: Tudor townsfolk & farmers
Frame: squared oak + wooden pegs
Walls: wattle and daub panels
On stilts? No — but jettied floors
Roof: thatch or clay tiles
See it: Little Moreton Hall; Lavenham
Wow! Some of these houses are over 500 years old and still lived in today. Little Moreton Hall leans over so much it looks like it might tip — but it has stood for centuries!
3Middle Ages → today

German Fachwerk

📍 Germany & Alsace — whole towns of timber-framed houses
Whole streets in Germany are built this way — the patterns of timber are almost like decoration.

🔍 Spot it!

  • A Wattle-and-daub (or clay-wrapped sticks) filling every panel
  • B Timbers set in beautiful criss-cross patterns
  • C Steep roofs for snow and rain

📖 The story

Germany is the world champion of timber-framed houses — it has probably more of them than anywhere else on Earth. The German name is Fachwerk, which means “framework.”

Builders loved to show off, arranging the timbers in fancy patterns. The gaps were filled with wattle and daub, or with clever clay-wrapped sticks called Lehmwickel — little “mud sausages” twisted around a stave and stacked up.

Who: German & French townsfolk
Frame: timber in patterns
Walls: wattle & daub / mud sausages
On stilts? No
Roof: steep tiles
See it: Quedlinburg; Colmar
Wow! There is a road in Germany called the Timber-Frame Road (Fachwerkstraße) that links dozens of pretty towns full of these houses — hundreds of miles long!
4from about 1,200 years ago

Japanese Farmhouse

📍 Japan — bamboo-and-earth walls on a raised wooden floor
A traditional Japanese house sits on short posts, with walls of woven bamboo plastered with layers of earth.

🔍 Spot it!

  • A Woven bamboo lattice instead of hazel — then earth plaster
  • B A big, beautiful curved roof
  • C The whole floor raised on posts, off the damp ground

📖 The story

In Japan, builders weave a lattice from bamboo tied with rope, then spread on layers of earth plaster — a rough coat first, then finer and finer coats until it is beautifully smooth. It's the same idea as wattle and daub, done with the plants that grow in Japan.

Japanese houses sit up on wooden posts, lifting the floor above the damp. The bendy earth walls are brilliant in an earthquake — they can sway a little instead of cracking apart.

Who: Japanese farming families
Weave: bamboo, tied with rope
Walls: layers of earth plaster
On stilts? Yes — a raised floor
Roof: thick thatch or tiles
See it: Himeji Castle; old minka
Wow! Even mighty Himeji Castle — a giant white fortress — has walls built the wattle-and-daub way: woven bamboo packed with earth, then painted white.
51800s → today

Colombian Bahareque

📍 Colombia — the coffee country, built from giant bamboo
In Colombia's coffee towns, bahareque houses are painted in bright, happy colours.

🔍 Spot it!

  • A Woven cane packed with mud — wattle and daub again!
  • B Posts of guadua, a giant, super-strong bamboo
  • C Bright paint and wide roofs for the rain

📖 The story

In the mountains of Colombia, where the coffee grows, people build with guadua — a giant bamboo as strong as some metals. They make a frame and woven walls of cane, then pack it with mud. This is called bahareque.

It has a superpower: it is brilliant in earthquakes. The light, bendy walls sway and hold together when the ground shakes. After a terrible earthquake in 1999, many homes were rebuilt this way on purpose — proving a 6,000-year-old idea is still one of the best.

Who: Colombian coffee families
Frame: guadua giant bamboo
Walls: woven cane + mud plaster
Superpower: earthquake-safe
Roof: tiles, wide overhang
See it: Salamina; Filandia
Wow! The whole coffee region is a UNESCO World Heritage site, and its bright bahareque houses are a big part of why. A house made of bamboo and mud — treasured by the world!

Part Three
How to Build One
— step by step

This is how builders really did it, in order, from the ground up. Follow the numbers. Each step has a picture to show you what's happening.

Building the stilts & the frame

1Drive in the stilts

the legs go first

Choose strong, straight posts — oak is best. Drive them deep into the ground so they can't wobble. Old trick: burn the buried ends black in a fire first — the charred wood resists rot and insects for years longer. Set four corner posts (plus a middle one for a big house).

2Build the platform & frame

a floor in the air

Lay strong beams across the tops of the stilts to make a raised floor platform. On top, stand up the wall frame: corner posts and a top beam. The joints are cut to lock together and pinned with wooden pegs — no nails needed. Now you have the skeleton of the house.

3Fix the staves

the uprights

Inside each wall panel, fit slim upright sticks called staves. Their tops slot into little holes drilled in the beam above, and their bottoms sit in a groove below. Space them out with room to weave between. These are what the wattle will grab onto.

Weaving, daubing & finishing

4Weave the wattle

the fun part!

Take long, bendy branches (withies of hazel or willow) and weave them in and out between the staves — over, under, over, under — exactly like weaving a basket. Push each row down tight against the last. Fill the whole panel with a strong, woven web.

5Mix the daub

make the mud

Mix clay soil + a little sand + lots of chopped straw + water into a thick, sticky paste. People squished it together with their bare feet (fun and messy!). Don't skip the straw — it is the glue that stops the wall cracking. A rough recipe: about 4 scoops clay, 1 sand, 1 straw.

6Press on the daub

squish it in

Grab handfuls of daub (builders called each ball a “cat”) and press them into the woven wattle — from both sides at once, so they squeeze together through the gaps and lock on as one solid wall. Smooth the surface flat with your hands.

7Let it dry

be patient

As the water leaves, the mud shrinks a little and small cracks appear — that's completely normal! Dry it slowly (fast drying makes big cracks). Then fill the cracks with a little more daub. More straw in the mix means fewer cracks next time.

8Paint on the lime wash

the raincoat

When the wall is hard and dry, scratch it a little so the next coat grips, then brush on lime wash — a thin white coat that keeps out rain and mould but still lets the wall breathe. This protects the daub and gives the house its bright white look.

9Add the roof

a straw hat

Build a steep roof frame and cover it with thatch — thick bundles of straw or reed, laid in overlapping rows from the bottom up so rain runs straight off. Make it steep and thick and it will keep the house dry for many years.

10Look after it

it lasts if you care

A wattle-and-daub house lives a long time if you look after it: re-paint the lime wash now and then, patch any cracks with fresh daub, and check the thatch. Keep the water out and the wall can last hundreds of years — some Tudor houses still stand today!

Part Four
Build Your Own
50 cm Model

Now it's Arthur's turn! Here is how to make a half-metre model house on stilts, using safe, easy craft materials — doing exactly what real builders did, just smaller.

Arthur's model — the plan
You're a real builder now. Weaving sticks and squishing mud into them is exactly what people did 6,000 years ago. If your wall cracks, don't worry — real houses cracked too! Just add more straw and let it dry slowly.
⚠ Grown-up jobs: cutting the dowels or skewers, using a craft knife, and the hot-glue gun are for an adult. Arthur's jobs: weaving, mixing the mud, daubing, painting and thatching — the best bits!

ABase & stilts

Use a shoebox, plywood or foam board as the ground. You can part-fill a box with sand or soil and push the stilts in — just like real marshy ground! Stilts = bamboo skewers or thin dowels, about 10–12 cm tall. Use 4 corners (+1 middle). Check the tops are level before the glue sets — a blob of playdough at each foot holds them straight.

BFloor & frame

Lay lolly (craft) sticks across the stilt tops to make the floor, then build a simple box frame on top: four corner uprights and a top rail. Glue with PVA (safe and slow) or ⚠ hot glue with an adult. For the real look, wrap string or garden wire around the joints, like the lashings old builders used.

CWeave the wattle

Glue in upright sticks (staves) about 2–3 cm apart. Then weave something bendy in and out: thin twigs, garden wire, raffia, string, or real willow prunings. Tip: soak twigs in water first so they bend without snapping. Pack the weave tight so the mud has something to grip.

Arthur's model — the mud & the finish

DMix the daub

A mix that really sticks (and won't crumble):

2 parts soil or air-dry clay • 1 part sand • 1 part chopped dried grass / straw • 1 part PVA glue + a little water. Stir to thick peanut-butter texture. The PVA is the magic ingredient — it dries hard and stops crumbling.
Easy option: just use air-dry clay with some grass pressed in.

EDaub it on

Press small balls of mud into the weave from both sides, squishing them together through the gaps so they lock on. Smooth it with wet fingers. Leave the frame sticks showing for the classic Tudor half-timbered look. (Keep mud off the glued joints until they've set.)

FFinish & roof

When it's fully dry (1–3 days), paint the walls white (poster or acrylic paint, or PVA + white paint) to look like lime wash, and paint the frame sticks dark brown for the black-and-white look. Make a card or balsa roof and glue on dried grass, raffia or straw in overlapping rows from the bottom up — your thatch!

Builder's words to know

Wattle
The woven basket of sticks — the wall's skeleton.
Daub
The sticky mud plaster (clay + sand + straw + water) squished into the wattle.
Stave
An upright stick fixed in the frame that you weave around.
Withy
A long, bendy branch (hazel or willow) woven sideways through the staves.
“Cat”
A ball of daub pushed into the wattle. (A funny old builder's word!)
Lime wash
A thin white coat painted over the wall to keep rain off while still letting it breathe.
Cob
A cousin of wattle and daub — walls of mud and straw, but with no woven skeleton inside.
Half-timbering
A house where a wooden frame holds everything up and the panels between are filled in.
Pile / stilt
A tall post that lifts the house up off the ground.
Thatch
A roof made of dry straw or reed.
Jetty
An upper floor that sticks out over the floor below.
Dendrochronology
Dating old wood by counting its tree-rings — how we know the age of the lake villages.
Guadua
A giant, super-strong bamboo used to build in Colombia.
Fachwerk
The German word for a timber-framed house (“framework”).
